# Trust Model

## Objective

The Martians Protocol should reduce unnecessary trust and unnecessary disclosure without pretending either can be eliminated from family life.

The protocol's goal is not to make all history public. Its goal is to make material institutional claims **provable to the people who legitimately need to verify them**.

## Trust domains

### Human trust

Some claims inherently require people: kinship attestations, mentorship, caregiving, contextual contribution, dispute resolution, and governance judgment.

### Institutional trust

Legal ownership, corporate authority, identity documents, court orders, trusts, estates, and regulated financial records may depend on external institutions and jurisdictions.

### Cryptographic trust

Signatures, hashes, commitments, multisignature policies, append-only event structures, and Merkle proofs can provide integrity, provenance, authorization evidence, and tamper detection.

They prove what was signed, committed, or recorded; they do not automatically prove that an underlying factual claim is legally or factually true.

### Proof-infrastructure trust

The private Big Book establishes institutional proof history. The public Little Book establishes selected external credibility. The Vault preserves governed source evidence.

A future permissioned Big Book can reduce single-operator trust when nodes are controlled by genuinely independent parties such as trustees, auditors, custodians, or separate trusts/institutions.

Multiple nodes under one person's control are operational redundancy, not decentralized institutional trust.

### Protocol trust

Smart contracts or shared services may enforce deterministic state transitions where their assumptions are appropriate. They are optional implementation mechanisms, not constitutional legitimacy.

## Trust minimization rules

- Do not trust one administrator to rewrite material history.
- Do not trust one key with irreversible control over critical functions.
- Do not treat a blockchain as proof that an off-chain factual claim is true.
- Do not expose confidential source material merely to increase verifiability.
- Do not give a verifier more information than is necessary to verify the legitimate claim.
- Do not equate family membership with unrestricted access to family history.
- Do not equate a Big Book commitment with permission to inspect the underlying Big Book records.
- Do not allow the Little Book to become a metadata side channel capable of reconstructing private history.
- Require stronger attestation thresholds as consequence and irreversibility increase.
- Preserve evidence provenance and source-version digests.
- Preserve correction, revocation, and supersession history without immortalizing unnecessary private details.

## Privacy trust boundaries

### Participant versus participant

One participant may be entitled to verify their own contribution, agreement, authority, vote, or entitlement while having no right to inspect another participant's equivalent records.

### Family versus enterprise

A family-network role does not automatically grant access to an independently governed enterprise's confidential records.

### Child/guardian/successor

Identity continuity and succession must not imply that every successor receives every historical confidential record. Access to sensitive history remains policy-scoped.

### External verifier

Banks, courts, partners, auditors, investors, or counterparties should receive proof of the requested claim rather than unrestricted access to the institution whenever possible.

## Threats to design against

- compromised administrator credentials;
- lost private keys;
- colluding attestators;
- false contribution claims;
- unauthorized disclosure of family records;
- insider curiosity and privilege creep;
- metadata reconstruction from public proofs;
- governance capture;
- token-based plutocracy leaking into family authority;
- founder overreach;
- succession failure;
- ambiguous ownership claims;
- retrospective history rewriting;
- immutable preservation of unnecessary harmful/private data;
- cross-network privilege escalation;
- smart-contract bugs;
- malicious or accidental protocol upgrades; and
- dependency on one vendor, application, operator, blockchain, or archive.

## Core principle

> **Cryptography protects integrity and authorization. Governance establishes legitimate authority. Evidence supports factual claims. Privacy limits disclosure. Law governs legal rights. These must not be confused.**
